Currently reading. I have finished Book I and am beginning Book II.

## First reaction

Book I is much stranger—and so far much less convincing—than I expected. Its account of crime, punishment, property, and social conditions can sound surprisingly proto-Marxian or proto-Foucauldian, centuries before either thinker. The dialogue is satirical, which complicates any straightforward attribution of its claims to More, but the voice still often strikes me as arrogant and self-satisfied.

## Theft and punishment

The discussion of capital punishment for theft contains a sharp incentive argument: if theft and murder receive the same punishment, a thief has reason to kill witnesses. But the broader account treats criminal behavior as largely produced by circumstances and by a society that failed to educate and provide for people properly. It feels remarkably modern—almost like a contemporary “blame the system” argument—and, at least in Book I, too sweeping to persuade me.

## Property and governance

The diagnosis of private property as the central obstacle to a well-functioning society seems close to the opposite of true to me. More generally, many of the claims currently feel like provocations prepared for the next dinner party rather than a serious engagement with the second-order problems of governing a society.

So far, this is disappointing relative to my expectations. Book II turns to the detailed description of Utopia itself; perhaps that is where the argument becomes richer or more interesting.
