    All six indices in one chart · by index weight

    Read a line left to right: at any year on the x-axis, its height is the share of that index’s weight founded in or before that year. A line that climbs early belongs to an old index; one that stays low until the 1970s–90s is a young index. The dashed vertical line is your current cutoff — everything to its left is the weight the filter flatlines. CAC 40, FTSE 100 and Nikkei 225 are drawn from their five founding-era cohorts, so their curves are interpolated across the cohort bands (1900, 1950, 1970, 1990) rather than built from individual companies. The curves are lightly smoothed; the table below keeps the exact shares.

    How the adjustment works

    The rule

    Each constituent keeps its index weight for the whole period. If its founding year is before the cutoff, its price is frozen at the 1980 level — a 0% return every year — while every younger company compounds as modelled.

    filtered(t) = Σyoung wi · Pi(t)  +  Σold wi · 100

    Founding year = economic origin

    A spin-off, demerger or privatisation does not reset the clock. The year shown is when the underlying business began, even if the listed vehicle is much younger — which is why the “listed” column in the tables below is separate, and often decades later.

    Today’s constituents are back-filled over the whole period (a survivorship-biased simplification, fine for a mockup, worth stating out loud).

    Examples — lineage calls in this data

    • Siemens Energy — 1847, not 2020. The spin-off inherited Siemens’ power business; the firm is 19th-century, the listing isn’t.
    • Deutsche Post (DHL) — 1947, not the 2000 IPO. Traced to the Deutsche Bundespost, founded in the British zone in 1947.

    About this mockup. Index paths are modelled from approximate historical year-end levels (S&P 500, Dow Jones Industrial Average, DAX performance index, CAC 40, FTSE 100, Nikkei 225), rebased to January 1980 = 100 and log-interpolated between anchor years. Individual company paths are stylised: each constituent is given a persistent out- or under-performance tilt versus its index, rescaled so the weighted average reproduces the index exactly. The S&P 500 filter runs on all 500 constituents, each modelled individually — the 70 largest, an extended tier of newer entrants and mid-caps (Robinhood, Coinbase, AppLovin, DoorDash…), and the full remaining tail of 397 smaller constituents — with weights summing to 100%; for the CAC 40, FTSE 100 and Nikkei 225 the filter still uses five founding-era cohorts per index rather than full constituent lists. Founding years reflect economic origin as described above. Nothing here is a backtest or investment advice.

    Drag the cutoff and watch 1989 Japan, the dot-com years and the post-2010 US tech run change character — that, in one slider, is the argument about how much of “the market” is really a bet on old companies.
